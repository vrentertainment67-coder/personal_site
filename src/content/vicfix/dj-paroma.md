DJ Paroma — Paroma Chatterjee — grew up in Thane in a house that wasn't peaceful, stopped studying after school because the money was needed then and not later, and at 18 was flying as cabin crew — one of six taken from 200 who interviewed. Three years in, her father died while she was on a flight on the other side of the world. She came home, handed back the uniform, the licenses, the entry pass — everything tying her to that life — and stopped earning completely for eight months to work out what came next. What she decided was music, in a world where she knew nobody. She got a number for a DJ she'd never met, rang him to ask where to learn, and broke a fixed deposit to pay the fees. Her teacher — who'd taught 200-plus students — wrote that she knew the least of anyone when she walked in, and she left with the highest score the institute had ever recorded. Then she took the job that paid ₹4,000 a month and played to a club with no crowd in it for eight months.\n\nFifteen years later: official Bollywood remixes in two film industries, a national-television jury seat, runner-up for Best Bollywood DJ in the country two years running — in an open category, males and females both — and close to a million people following her. She built it on weddings, corporate stages and broadcast, not the club circuit, and got interviewed endlessly about being a woman who does the job and almost never about the job itself. The template for that came from her mother, a working woman in a male-dominated field long before it was common: the first thing Paroma learned was that being the woman in the room isn't the big deal everyone assumes it is.\n\nSo this is the conversation the title promises — everyone calls themselves a celebrity DJ now, a month or two in, and she has thoughts on a name that's supposed to be earned. It's why she stripped 'celebrity' and 'influencer' out of her own bio and put 'producer' in; why, with a following that size, there are still only two songs that are actually hers, and why she's deliberately holding the rest back until the scene settles. The DJ-award racket where you're asked to pay for the trophy after your name's already been called. And Saheli — 'a female friend' — the women-first venture she launched quietly with no press release in Bangalore and Kochi, built to give a trained-but-unbooked local female DJ from each city her first real break. Built by someone who had every reason to choose the safe thing, and didn't."
